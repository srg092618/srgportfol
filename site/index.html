Featured project · epicast

Disease forecasting for malaria, HIV and Ebola disease in Africa

An end-to-end forecasting pipeline: it downloads public WHO, UNAIDS and outbreak data, cleans it into one table, forecasts with eight model families, scores every model on years and weeks it had not seen, recalibrates the uncertainty intervals and builds the interactive dashboard below.

0.58

HIV forecast error relative to a naive baseline, from 6,499 backtest forecasts. Lower is better.

94%

Share of outcomes that landed inside the 95% intervals after recalibration.

8

Model families, from a random walk to gradient boosting and a renewal-equation outbreak model.

83

Automated tests, including one that checks the backtest never sees the future.

Live dashboard. Map, country forecasts, outbreak replay and accuracy tables. It runs entirely in your browser.

How it works

Every download is pinned to a fixed version and logged with a hash, so a run can be reproduced exactly. Models forecast nine quantiles on a log scale. A rolling-origin backtest hides each future year or week, fits, forecasts and compares, and scores the result with the weighted interval score. Conformal recalibration then widens intervals that were too confident, using only outcomes known at the time.

For outbreaks, a renewal-equation model with time-varying R forecasts weekly cases, and a Poisson base rate gives the 12-month chance of a new Ebola, Sudan or Bundibugyo virus outbreak.

What the backtest showed

HIV: one gradient-boosting model pooled across 147 countries cut forecast error by about 40% compared with "next year equals this year".

Malaria: no model beat that naive baseline, because incidence has stalled since 2015. The dashboard reports this rather than hiding it.

Outbreaks: at one to four weeks ahead, beating persistence is hard. The renewal model edges ahead on Bundibugyo 2026, but 256 forecasts are too few to call it conclusive.

Python, pandas, NumPy, SciPy, statsmodels, scikit-learn, Shapely and pytest.

A portfolio project, not a tool for operational decisions. For public-health decisions, use WHO, Africa CDC and national ministries of health.

Visualization · Random forests and clustering

From forest to clusters: 64-dimensional data in 3D

A random forest of 200 trees learns to read 1,797 handwritten digits, each a 64-pixel vector. The 3D scene walks through five stages: a PCA projection, the trees themselves, the forest's own proximity space, scoring on held-out digits, and k-means clustering with no labels at all.

97.6%

Accuracy on 540 held-out digits the forest never trained on.

97.0%

Out-of-bag accuracy, an internal check that agrees with the held-out score.

13

Errors out of 540. Every one was a low-confidence call, mostly 5s, 8s and 9s.

0.665

Adjusted Rand index for k-means against the true digits (1 is a perfect match).

Interactive 3D. Drag to rotate, hover a point for details and use the stage bar along the bottom to move through the pipeline.

About

Five years of data science, and a habit of checking the source

I'm Steven Goncalves, a data scientist in New Jersey who specializes in machine learning. Over five years of studying and practicing data science I've built recommendation systems, customer segmentation and predictive models, and helped tune a neural network that reads X-rays.

The work I care most about is the kind where a number changes a decision: which patients a radiologist sees first, how much stock a business orders, where an outbreak is likely to grow. That's why every model I ship comes with a baseline it has to beat, a test on data it has never seen and an honest range around its answer.

Before data science I worked as a multimedia journalist covering politics and international affairs. It taught me to verify before publishing and to write for readers who aren't specialists, and I still work that way: plain-language results, with the math available to anyone who wants it.
